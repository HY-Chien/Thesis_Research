
\chapter{Experiments}
\label{ch:exp}

This chapter evaluates the final SEEK system under the same honest wiki-21M retrieval protocol used by DeepRAG and compares it with representative reasoning and adaptive-retrieval baselines. After the main results, the chapter examines why the system works: whether decomposition actually improves retrieval, how much of the remaining MuSiQue gap comes from wrong intermediate answers rather than from decomposition wording, and whether retrieval or the confidence gate could be removed without losing accuracy. The later sections then look at the two answer paths in more detail. They measure how often each path is selected and where each path fails, compare SEEK's errors with DeepRAG's on the same questions, and ask how far tail-leakage surprisal can be trusted as a confidence signal.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate on HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, and PopQA. HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, and MuSiQue test multi-hop reasoning, while PopQA tests single-hop factual recall and exposes over-decomposition on simple entity-centric questions. To align with DeepRAG~\cite{deeprag}, all headline results use the first 1{,}000 questions of each development set without shuffling.

\subsection{Retrieval Corpus}

The main comparison uses the honest open-domain corpus: the shared Elasticsearch \texttt{wiki} index over \texttt{psgs\_w100}, containing approximately 21M Wikipedia passages. We use BM25 with top-$k=8$ for HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, and MuSiQue, and top-$k=3$ for PopQA. SEEK applies a path-aware title policy: the direct path retrieves with the original question over paragraph text only, while the decomposition path retrieves short sub-question queries over title and paragraph text. This policy is dataset-agnostic because it depends only on query type.

The final system further uses \emph{original-question evidence union}. For each decomposed sub-question, the evidence context is the union of passages retrieved by the sub-question and passages retrieved by the original question. This preserves the global context that can be lost when a long question is decomposed into short sub-questions.

\subsection{Metrics and Baselines}

We report SQuAD-style exact match (EM) and token-level F1 after standard normalisation. When a dataset provides aliases, the maximum score over aliases is used. The main table includes representative Llama-3-8B baselines reported by DeepRAG~\cite{deeprag}: CoT and CoT-Retrieve as reasoning baselines, FLARE~\cite{flare} and DRAGIN~\cite{dragin} as training-free adaptive retrieval baselines, and DeepRAG as the strongest learned adaptive baseline. Internal comparisons include the path-aware baseline, the decomposition-path original-question evidence union ablation, and the final SEEK system with type-aware verifier replay.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

All SEEK results use Llama-3-8B-Instruct with the 12K three-source LoRA decomposer trained on MuSiQue, 2WikiMultihopQA, and PopQA. The adapter uses rank 16, alpha 32, and dropout 0.05 on all linear modules, trained in bf16 with a 1024-token context for 3 epochs using LLaMA-Factory~\cite{llamafactory}, with effective batch size 16 and learning rate $1\times10^{-4}$ under cosine decay with 10\% warmup; training takes about 38 minutes on one A6000 GPU. At inference, the adapter is enabled for decomposition and sub-question answering and disabled for synthesis, and the decomposition path uses the tail-leakage surprisal gate at $\tau=0.0085$. The final verifier uses the rule set of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} with tail-leakage surprisal as its confidence signal, plus a conservative type-aware fallback. This fallback changes only the final choice between the direct and decomposed candidates; it does not rerun generation and does not train an additional model.

Several ablations and diagnostic analyses in this chapter were carried out on development traces produced while the system was being built, some of them before the gate and verifier signals changed from maximum token entropy to tail-leakage surprisal. Their absolute numbers can therefore differ slightly from the final configuration, but each table is internally consistent and the comparisons within it are unaffected.

\subsection{Gold and Base Decomposition on MuSiQue}
\label{sec:gold-decomposition-recall}

To separate the value of decomposition from the value of the trained decomposer, we run a focused MuSiQue retrieval-recall analysis using the first 1{,}000 development questions. This slice contains only 2-hop questions, which makes the official MuSiQue gold decomposition directly comparable with SEEK's predicted two-step chain. Table~\ref{tab:musique-gold-recall} reports answer-containing hit@5 under the same wiki-21M and path-aware retrieval condition as Table~\ref{tab:answer-recall}.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption[MuSiQue Gold and Base Decomposition Recall]{MuSiQue answer-containing hit@5 for original, SEEK, gold, and untuned-base decompositions.}
    \label{tab:musique-gold-recall}
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{ l p{6.5cm} c }
        \toprule
        Arm & Interpretation & Hit@5 \\
        \midrule
        Original question & No decomposition & 19.0 \\
        SEEK & SEEK decomposition with predicted intermediate answer & 38.8 \\
        Gold & Official MuSiQue decomposition with gold intermediate answer & \textbf{55.8} \\
        Gold raw & Official decomposition without replacing \texttt{\#1} & 19.8 \\
        SEEK + gold bridge & SEEK sub-question text with gold intermediate answer & 54.1 \\
        Untuned base + gold bridge & Llama-3-8B-Instruct without LoRA, using gold intermediate answer & 52.9 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The gap between original retrieval and SEEK retrieval is large: 19.0 to 38.8 hit@5. The oracle gold chain reaches 55.8, showing that decomposition has further retrieval headroom. However, the important diagnostic is that \emph{Gold raw} collapses back to the original-query level. A 2-hop decomposition is useful only when the first-hop bridge entity is correctly resolved and substituted into the second query. SEEK's first-hop intermediate-answer EM is 41.9\%. When the first hop is answered correctly, SEEK reaches 56.4 hit@5, essentially matching the gold chain on the same subset at 58.5. When the first hop is wrong, SEEK falls to 25.9 while gold stays at 53.8, so these questions are not intrinsically harder and their evidence remains reachable with the right bridge entity. The SEEK--gold gap is therefore almost entirely bridge-answer drift rather than poor decomposition wording.

The untuned base model also clarifies why the decomposer is trained. With the same prompt and no LoRA adapter, the base model produces a valid two-step decomposition for only 149 of 1{,}000 MuSiQue examples and has a 15.3\% format-parsing failure rate. Its query text can retrieve well under oracle bridge substitution, but it is not structurally reliable enough to deploy. LoRA training therefore buys step-count control, valid placeholders, and compact BM25-friendly query style, not merely better wording.

Three patterns account for most of the substantive losses. The first is over-decomposition on 2Wiki comparison questions, where 39 of the 160 SEEK wins involve DeepRAG chains of five or more steps. A representative case asks which film has the earlier-born director, \emph{Buffet Froid} or \emph{Lost in Africa}. DeepRAG produces six steps but retrieves only once: it resolves the first director correctly, then answers the remaining steps from parametric memory, hallucinates the second director, and finally appends two redundant comparison steps that restate the original question. The wrong parametric comparison in those terminal steps flips an otherwise recoverable chain. SEEK's decomposer emits exactly the four factual lookups, retrieves for each, and leaves the comparison to the synthesis stage.

The second pattern is a decomposition spiral. DeepRAG generates each follow-up question conditioned on the chain answered so far, so a wrong intermediate answer can rewrite the rest of the plan. In one HotpotQA case, the model resolves the stage name Aladin to the wrong person and then spends five further steps trying to make that person fit the question, with later follow-up fields visibly containing fragments of the earlier erroneous reasoning. This contamination appears in roughly a quarter to a third of the SEEK-win cases on HotpotQA and MuSiQue. SEEK's decomposition is produced up front from the original question alone, so a wrong bridge answer degrades one retrieval query but cannot corrupt the structure of the remaining sub-questions.

The third pattern is structural mis-decomposition. Asked who sings \emph{Home Alone Tonight} with the singer of another song, DeepRAG splits the question into two independent singer lookups, drops the duet relation, and returns the same artist for both. SEEK's placeholder binding keeps the relation explicit in the second sub-question and recovers the duet partner. On 2Wiki, an additional dataset-specific weakness appears: in 55 of the 160 SEEK wins, DeepRAG's retrieve-or-answer policy chose the parametric option at every step and hallucinated the whole chain. SEEK's tail-leakage gate keeps step-level retrieval near or above 90\% on the multi-hop datasets (Table~\ref{tab:rag-necessity}), so a fully parametric chain is rare.

These counts should be read with two caveats. About 15\% to 21\% of the SEEK-win set are format near-misses in which DeepRAG's answer is essentially correct but fails exact match, such as \emph{Murray Hill, New Jersey} against the gold \emph{Murray Hill}. The comparison is also symmetric: within its own win set, SEEK's decomposition is sometimes poor as well, and the question is rescued by the direct path or by original-question evidence union rather than by the decomposition itself. On HotpotQA, 29 of the 98 SEEK wins come from the direct candidate. The DeepRAG-only column contains failures of the mirrored kind, which is consistent with the dual-path design rather than evidence against it.

\section{Tail-Leakage Surprisal as a Confidence Signal}
\label{sec:entropy-analysis}

Tail-leakage surprisal is the signal the deployed gate relies on, so the final analysis asks how far it can be trusted. In the offline gate replay it is the strongest of the tested retrieval-gate signals: at the deployed threshold, which skips the most confident tenth of decomposed steps, it keeps the four-dataset average at 40.60 EM, while a maximum-entropy gate at the matched retrieval rate reaches 40.45. These results support tail-leakage surprisal as a sub-question-level retrieval gate.

The difference is clearer when the signals are scored as error detectors. We ran a battery of more than forty confidence signals on the direct path in the deployed setting, covering temperature-scaled and R\'enyi entropies, log-probability statistics, top-$k$ mass and margin scores, self-consistency agreement, P(True)-style self-evaluation, and z-scored combinations, evaluated on the full 1{,}000-question traces of all four datasets with paired bootstrap over 1{,}000 resamples. Table~\ref{tab:signal-battery} lists the strongest signals, scored by the AUROC of predicting a wrong direct answer. Tail-leakage surprisal has the best macro AUROC at 0.749 and beats the strongest alternative, minimum top-five mass, on every dataset, with the paired difference significant at $P<0.001$. Temperature-2 entropy is stronger on 2Wiki and PopQA but collapses to 0.636 on MuSiQue, while maximum token entropy stays at 0.726.

The battery also gives the decomposition argument of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} an empirical check. Scoring each chain-rule component of token entropy separately, the escape term alone reaches a macro AUROC of 0.742, ambiguity within the top-five candidates reaches 0.712, and the composition of the tail reaches 0.502, barely above chance. Full entropy mixes the three and lands at 0.726, while summing the escape surprisal over the answer reaches 0.749. Summation contributes the rest of the margin: on single-token answers the sum and the weakest-token maximum coincide, but on answers of seven or more tokens the sum reaches an AUROC of 0.805 while the weakest-token aggregate stays at 0.788, because wrong answers tend to leak small amounts of mass at several positions rather than a large amount at one.

At the same time, tail-leakage surprisal is not a correctness guarantee. Across the pooled traces, 491 wrong direct answers carry surprisal at or below the median of correct answers: 22\% of the wrong answers on 2Wiki, 17\% on HotpotQA, 16\% on MuSiQue, and 7\% on PopQA. More than half of these confidently wrong answers are clean parametric hallucinations produced with essentially zero leakage, and another 148 are alias or boundary near-misses whose answer is essentially right but fails exact match. No threshold can reach the first group, because nothing in the token distribution marks the answer as wrong.

The AUROC of 0.782 should be read as a population-level statistic rather than as the accuracy of a per-question detector. The medians are well separated, with pooled values of 0.27 for both-correct and 1.12 for both-wrong, but the tails overlap heavily: on every dataset, the tenth percentile of the both-wrong group lies below the median of the both-correct group. This also explains why moving the verifier onto the new signal changes little: replacing the development-stage entropy thresholds with tail-leakage thresholds shifts the four-dataset average by less than 0.2 EM, within the replay noise, so the final system adopts the tail-leakage version mainly to keep a single confidence statistic throughout. The ranking gain of tail-leakage surprisal lies in the uncertain tail of the answer distribution, while the verifier's binary rules act on the confident head, where the two signals agree.

The decomposition-path signal is considerably weaker at the question level. Rerunning the battery on the decomposed answers gives tail-leakage surprisal a macro AUROC of only 0.631, essentially tied with the best alternatives, and on 2Wiki the strongest signal reaches only 0.587, close to random, because many 2Wiki parametric errors are produced with full confidence. The step-level signal remains informative as a retrieval gate, which is how SEEK uses it, but it says little about whether the final decomposed answer is right.

This is why SEEK deploys tail-leakage surprisal as a per-step retrieval gate and keeps confidence as only one signal inside a dual-path verifier, rather than treating any confidence score as a global router. Confidence helps estimate whether a path may be reliable; it does not reliably identify which path is correct when the two paths disagree.

\section{Summary}

Under the honest wiki-21M protocol, SEEK exceeds DeepRAG on the four-dataset average and wins on 2Wiki, MuSiQue, and PopQA, with HotpotQA remaining the only dataset below DeepRAG. Decomposition is the main source of this result. It raises answer-containing recall strongly on the multi-hop datasets, and the gold MuSiQue decomposition shows that the recall ceiling extends to 55.8 hit@5, with most of the remaining gap explained by wrong intermediate answers rather than by decomposition wording. Retrieval stays necessary after decomposition, since removing it costs 9.3 to 27.3 EM, and the confidence gate cannot take decomposition's place: a direct-only confidence-gated baseline stays 12.3 to 28.6 EM below decompose-with-gate on 2Wiki, MuSiQue, and PopQA even with a retrospectively chosen threshold.

The analyses also mark the limits of each component. The confidence gate behaves as an efficiency mechanism rather than a correctness guarantee: gated retrieval nearly matches always-retrieve accuracy while saving some retrieval calls. Tail-leakage surprisal ranks wrong answers better than entropy and every other signal in a battery of more than forty, yet confidently wrong answers survive any single threshold. The direct and decomposition paths are complementary, with oracle routing worth a further 3.8 to 6.1 EM. The case-level comparison with DeepRAG shows the same complementarity across systems: DeepRAG's chain-conditioned decomposition suffers from over-decomposition, error spirals, and zero-retrieval hallucination that SEEK's up-front decomposition and near-always sub-question retrieval avoid, while DeepRAG wins a comparable number of questions of its own. Both-wrong examples remain the largest error mass on every dataset and call for better retrieval and synthesis rather than better routing alone.
